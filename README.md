# freeze dried dog food: how to read the label, work out the real cost per day, and get money back on every bag

A 25-ounce bag of freeze-dried raw dog food can cost more than a 30-pound bag of decent kibble. That single fact is why this category makes people suspicious, and why it also quietly rewards anyone who does the maths before buying.

Freeze-dried is the most expensive format on the pet food shelf. It's also the one where most of the money gets lost to bad choices — buying a topper thinking it's a complete meal, judging value by bag price instead of calories, or paying full price every single month for a product you'll reorder for years.

Here's what actually matters, in the order it matters.

## Why the bag costs what it costs

The process is genuinely different from kibble, and it's worth knowing because it explains the price.

Freeze-drying happens in three steps. Ingredients are frozen first so the water in them is solid. Then a combination of low temperature and pressure turns that ice straight into vapour without passing through a liquid stage — that's sublimation. A final round of gentle drying raises the temperature to finish the job.

Because the food never gets cooked, the result ends up close to the original raw ingredients, minus the water. It's lightweight, shelf-stable without refrigeration, and doesn't need artificial preservatives. It also crumbles easily and, if your dog doesn't drink much at mealtime, needs rehydrating before it's ideal.

Air-dried is the format people confuse it with. That one uses low heat and circulating air over many hours. Texture ends up chewier, and depending on the brand the product may be closer to raw or lightly cooked. Both are shelf-stable; they are not the same thing.

None of this makes freeze-dried automatically better than a good kibble or a decent canned food. It's a different option with a different cost structure, and it works well when it's chosen for the right job.

## The label check that decides everything

Before price, before brand, before anything else: check whether the product is a **complete and balanced diet** or a **topper**.

Not every freeze-dried product carries an AAFCO nutritional adequacy statement. Plenty of them are designed and marketed as mixers, meal mixers, bowl boosters, or training treats. Fed as the main diet, those leave real gaps — you're paying premium prices for a supplement.

The label tells you, if you know where to look:

- **Look for the AAFCO statement.** If it says "formulated to meet the nutritional levels established by the AAFCO Dog Food Nutrient Profiles" for a specific life stage, it can function as a full diet. Stella & Chewy's Dinner Patties, for example, carry an all-life-stages statement.
- **Check the product name.** Words like *meal mixer*, *booster*, *mixer*, *topper*, and *treat* mean it's meant to sit alongside a base diet. Words like *dinner patties*, *meals*, *entrée*, or *complete* point the other way — but the AAFCO statement is the tiebreaker, not the name.
- **Check the guaranteed analysis.** Crude protein minimums in this category commonly run anywhere from around 36% to 46% on a dry-matter basis, with fat often between 22% and 35%. High fat means high calories, which matters more than you'd think for portion control.

A quick way to see the split in practice: on Chewy's freeze-dried dog food roundup, products sold as "Meal Mixers," "RawBoost Mixers," and "Bowl Boosters" sit in the same list as products sold as complete meals. They are not interchangeable.

> If you only take one thing from this article: buy a topper only if you want a topper, and pay topper prices on purpose.

Two things jump out.

Second, per-ounce pricing hides the actual cost, because calorie density varies a lot. A food at roughly 4,900 kcal/kg delivers far more energy per gram than one at 4,200 kcal/kg. ## Complete meal, topper, or training treat

Pick the role before you pick the brand. The three jobs have different budgets.

**Full diet.** Highest monthly cost, and the only option where the AAFCO statement is non-negotiable. A 25-pound dog on a complete freeze-dried diet eats roughly 3 to 4 ounces a day depending on the formula — around $6 to $11 a day at the prices above. That's the number to sanity-check against your budget, not the bag price.

**Topper.** You're using 10% or less of daily calories to boost palatability and nutrition. This is where freeze-dried earns its keep for picky eaters, and where a small bag genuinely lasts. A 4-ounce topper at $8.98 can stretch weeks if you're sprinkling, not pouring.

**Training treats.** Small pieces, high value, and the cheapest way to use the category. A 25-ounce bag of mini nibs used as rewards lasts a very long time.

Freeze-dried is calorie-dense, so overfeeding is easy and quiet — the volume looks small in the bowl. Portion by calories, then adjust based on body condition rather than the feeding chart alone.

## Feeding it properly

**Rehydration.** Ratios differ by brand, so read the bag. Stella & Chewy's suggests crumbling and adding about 1/4 cup of warm water per two patties. Primal's scoopable Pronto calls for roughly 1/3 cup of liquid per cup of food, and specifically recommends stainless steel, glass, or food-grade silicone bowls rather than plastic or porcelain, which are porous and trap germs. Bravo's freeze-dried dinners suggest soaking 10 to 15 minutes for more sensitive stomachs. Nulo's recipes can be served dry or with warm water or bone broth stirred through.

**Storage.** Unopened, freeze-dried is shelf-stable in a cool, dry place. Once rehydrated, treat it like fresh food — Primal specifies refrigerating and feeding within five days. Don't leave rehydrated portions sitting out.

**Transition.** A slow switch is standard advice for a reason. The usual schedule runs 25% new food to 75% old for one to three days, then 50/50 for two to three days, then 75/25 for two to three days, then all new food. Loose stools during the change are common and usually settle.

**Buy small first.** Freeze-dried is one of the few categories where palatability genuinely varies by dog, and a large bag of a novel protein you can't return is an expensive experiment.

## The handling rules nobody enjoys reading

Freeze-dried is raw or near-raw, which means pathogens are a real consideration — Salmonella and Listeria among them, with higher risk for puppies, seniors, immunocompromised dogs, and the humans handling the food.

Practical steps:

- Choose brands that apply a pathogen-reduction step such as high-pressure pasteurization, or that document tested ingredients.
- Treat it like raw meat. Wash hands, bowls, and surfaces after every feed.
- Keep young children and vulnerable household members away from the food and the dog's saliva right after eating.
- Check the life-stage statement if you're feeding a puppy or a senior — the calorie and nutrient needs are different, and puppies can need up to twice the standard portion.

## Quick answers

**Is freeze-dried better than kibble?** Not automatically. It retains more of the raw character and is far more convenient than frozen raw, but it's not a shortcut to better nutrition on its own. A complete-and-balanced freeze-dried diet, a complete-and-balanced kibble, and a complete-and-balanced canned food all meet the same AAFCO profiles.

**Can I feed it dry?** Yes. Many brands allow it, but dry feeding means your dog needs to drink enough water at mealtime. If they don't, rehydrate.

**How long does an opened bag last?** Shelf-stable and resealable bags keep well in a cool, dry place. Rehydrated portions don't — refrigerate and use within about five days.

**Which size bag should I start with?** The smallest one of the protein you're trialling. Freeze-dried acceptance is genuinely dog-specific.

**Does cashback work if I use Autoship?** On eligible items, yes — including subsequent refills. Prescription and pharmacy categories are excluded.
